In this introduction, we will list some basic facts about czech language that learner should be aware of, even though he does not need to establish a profound understanding of these concepts.

## You're acutally not learing one language, but two

Probably in most languages there is this concept of formal and informal language. However this gap between formal and informal language is so huge in Czech, that we often tend to think of them as different variants of the same language. The formal variant of Czech is being refered to as **Standart Czech** whereas the informal variant is being refered to as **Common Czech**.

The gap between Standart Czech and Common Czech doesn't consist only in vocabulary differences but also in the morphology. That means that special forms of a word that differ from the Standart Czech are being used merely every time. Note that this difference is between often ommited in the textbooks and Czech lessons and is being presented to learners only very later on in the process of learning czech, what I consider to be a big misconduct of Czech teachers.

The **Common Czech** is used in the oral production and online personal communication. Using the **Standart Czech** in the oral production is often being verbally punished by other speakers and is often considered as a weird think to do. The **Standart Czech** is in the other hand used in official written communication and politics. Note that even in very formal settings the **Common Czech** is still very likely to be used in the oral production. Also using the **Common Czech** in the written production usually feels very odd and it's usually done only in direct messages and amongst groups of friends. This very special place in the written production of **Standart Czech** is expressed well in it's Czech label which is **Spisovný** which could be loosely translated as **meant for writing**.

It is also important to mention that the **Common Czech** variants differ across Czech Republic and are a bit different in every region.

This topic of **Standart** and **Common Czech** is very huge and could go on and on, so the learner is encouraged to go into the unknow lands of a foreign language and build his own intuition of when to use what variant of the language.

The **Prague Common Czech** variants of words and patterns are presented in those materials in brackets, together with the **CC:** abbreviation. Despite them being in brackets, **do npt underestimate** their value and learn them as well.

## Free syntax

This topic is repeatedly disccused in these materials from various points of view but to give a little bit of introduction a lot of parts of the sentence are kinde free position wise. That means that notably subject, object and a verb of a sentence can be moreless anywhere. However the syntax is not entirely free, for instance **pre**position always needs to be in front of a word etc. This gets a bit delicate when presenting new constructions, since the construction is being presented in a certain way (certain wordorder), yet the wordordering of it can then differ a lot in various sentences.

## Ommited subject

The subject in Czech can be ommited and very frequently is. What more, telling the subject in every sentence often feels weird.

## Verb paradigmes
